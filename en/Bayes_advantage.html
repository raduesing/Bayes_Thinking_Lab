Why Bayes? — First the urn: where the posterior distribution comes from. Then the study: what a distribution over the parameter lets you answer.
What is this about?
Frequentist and Bayesian analyses often work with the same data and the same likelihood. They differ in what they treat as fixed and what as random. And therefore in which question they answer and how their result may be read. This tool shows this in two steps that build on each other.
Frequentists
The parameter is fixed (a fixed, unknown number); what is random is the data, because a new sample would look different.
Question: How plausible is my data if the parameter is like this?
Bayesians
The data are fixed (we have nothing more); what is uncertain is the parameter, which gets a probability distribution.
Question: How plausible are the possible parameter values, given that I have seen this data?
Why the urn first? The common thread
Tab 1, the urn: where the distribution comes from. We drew n times with replacement from an urn (we imagine the urn as huge, with practically infinitely many balls; only its unknown share of red p matters), and k of these draws were red. We ask: what can we learn from this data about the unknown share of red p in the urn? The two perspectives answer this differently:
Frequentists consider only one assumed urn and ask which draws it would deliver. In other words: what would future, fictitious data look like that were drawn from this urn again and again?
Bayesians instead imagine very many possible urns, with all proportions p from 0 to 1, and keep only those that produce exactly our draw. Some urns manage this often, others hardly ever, so they are differently plausible. The distribution of these “population urns” is exactly the posterior.

Tab 2, the study: what we do with this distribution. Because we have a distribution over the parameter, we can ask it anything. How likely is an effect above a threshold? Is it positive at all? Does it lie in a certain range? Each of these questions is simply an area under the curve (Act 1). Prior knowledge can be built in as well: the prior determines which urns we consider possible beforehand (Act 2). It is the same principle, only now it is about an effect β in BDI points instead of the proportion p of red balls.

Frequentists have no distribution over the parameter, only one urn. That is why a direct answer to such questions is out of reach for them. It is best to proceed in this order.
Colours
In both tabs: red = frequentist, blue = Bayesian (in tab 2 additionally prior green and likelihood orange). The balls in the urn are red and blue independently of this.

From the urn to regression

The urn example can be swapped out, for example for a regression coefficient b. The confidence interval then covers all values of b that are compatible with the data. It describes a procedure whose intervals contain the true value in 95% of cases over many samples. The credible interval, by contrast, names the most plausible population values that could have produced the observed coefficient (given data and prior). With informed priors the results diverge even further, try it above.
That was the idea: “many possible urns” become a distribution, the posterior. Because we have this distribution, we can also ask it completely different questions, not only about the most plausible value. Tab 2 shows what this looks like with a real study.

Act 1 Same Data — different questions possible
What am I seeing?
The curve, the so-called posterior distribution of β, shows which effect sizes are still plausible after taking prior knowledge (prior) and data into account. The blue area to the right of the chosen threshold shows how likely an effect above this boundary is.
What should I do?
Drag the threshold with the mouse and think about: from which effect size would the difference be substantively or clinically relevant to me? For example: should the effect be larger than 2, 2.5, or 5 BDI points?
How do I read the result?
The percentage directly answers the question: how likely is it that the true effect is larger than my chosen threshold? Example: P(β > 3) = 80% means: after taking the prior and the data into account, the probability that the effect is larger than 3 BDI points is 80%.
What's the difference to the frequentist analysis here?
A classical 95% confidence interval or a p-value does not answer this question directly. A p-value can, for example, show whether a result would be surprising under a specific null hypothesis. But it does not say: "How probable is it that the true effect is larger than 3?" With the posterior distribution, that probability can be read directly off the area under the curve.

Act 2 Prior as Cumulative Knowledge
What am I seeing?
Three curves: Prior (green, dashed — knowledge before the data), Likelihood (orange, dotted — what the data alone say) and Posterior (blue, filled — combined result). Below: a width comparison of 95% intervals.
What should I do?
Switch between non-informative and informative prior. The likelihood (data) stays identical — only the prior changes, and therefore the posterior.
How do I interpret this?
With an informative prior (10 prior studies: μ = 5, σ = 0.6) the posterior narrows considerably. Same data, more precise posterior — prior knowledge formally reduces uncertainty.
Why is this useful?
Frequentism structurally ignores prior knowledge. Bayes formalises cumulative learning: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. Scientific progress as an accumulated state of knowledge.

→ Frequentism has no formal mechanism for prior knowledge. With an informative prior — derived here from 10 prior studies — the posterior becomes substantially more precise: same data, narrower interval. That is cumulative learning in science. Note: The small difference between the frequentist CI and the Bayesian interval here must not be read as a methodological advantage of Bayes. The frequentist CI correctly uses the t-distribution because the residual spread is estimated from the data. For the Bayesian display, a normal approximation of the likelihood of β with SE = 2.10 is used for simplicity, to avoid needlessly complicating the illustration. Because of this different computation, the Bayesian interval is already marginally narrower under the non-informative prior and should not be counted as an advantage.

What does this tool show?
Frequentist and Bayesian analysis of the same data in two examples. Both use the same likelihood. The difference lies in what is fixed and what is random, which questions can be answered directly, and whether prior knowledge can enter formally. Tab 1 shows where the posterior comes from (many possible urns that fit the data), tab 2 what you can answer with it. Colours: red = frequentist, blue = Bayesian.
Tab 1 — The urn
n and k: number of draws (with replacement) and how often red came up. How many balls are in the urn does not matter, only the share p. Prior: Bayesian prior knowledge about the proportion p of red balls. Repetitions (frequentist) and Urns (Bayesian): size of the respective simulation. ↺ Simulate again re-rolls with the same settings.

Step 1 (frequentist): the urn is assumed known (p̂ = k/n), and draws are taken from it very often. Analytically this is the binomial distribution.
Step 2 (Bayesian): many urns with different p are rolled from the prior, and only those with exactly k red balls are kept. Their p values form the posterior, analytically a beta distribution.
Step 3: both solutions, analytic and simulated, side by side. The confidence interval is a Wilson interval (in R: prop.test(k, n, correct = FALSE)). Without correct = FALSE, prop.test() applies a continuity correction and returns a somewhat wider interval.
Tab 2 — Act 1: direct probabilities
Using a CBT study (n = 40, β = 4.80 BDI points, SE = 2.10): the posterior allows statements like P(β > 3) ≈ 80%, a direct probability about the parameter. A 95% confidence interval structurally cannot deliver that. Move the threshold on the curve and compare the three example questions below.
Tab 2 — Act 2: prior as cumulative knowledge
