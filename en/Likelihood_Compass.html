Two ways to a distribution: use the generating process if you know it, or decide pragmatically by model comparison.
Two ways to the right distribution
When choosing a likelihood, you can proceed in two ways. Both make sense; they just answer different questions.
Way 1: Use the generating process
Many distributions come from a concrete process. If you know this process for your data and it applies, the corresponding distribution is the right choice.

If you count the successes in a series of identical trials with the same success probability, you get a binomial distribution. If you count independent events that occur at a constant rate in a fixed period, you get a Poisson distribution; the higher the rate, the higher the mean. If you ask not for the number but for the waiting time until the k-th event, you end up with the Gamma distribution. So Poisson and Gamma describe the same process from two angles. If the rate rises, the waiting time gets shorter and the curve moves closer to zero.

The Inverse Gaussian describes the time until a process with drift and noise reaches a threshold, for example when someone accumulates evidence for a decision. The normal distribution arises from the sum of many small influences, the lognormal from their product, the ExGaussian from a normally distributed core process plus an exponential delay. The 📋 Cheat Sheet (above) names the mechanism for every distribution.
Reaction times show why shape alone is not enough: Lognormal, Gamma, Inverse Gaussian, and ExGaussian are all right-skewed, but they stand for different mechanisms (multiplicative, multi-step, drift to a threshold, normal plus delay).
Way 2: Compare pragmatically
Often you do not know the process exactly, or the data arise differently from what the distribution assumes. Then you look for a distribution that describes your data well enough and does not produce impossible values. It does not have to explain the process.

Sum scores from questionnaire items are a good example. Their shape resembles a binomial distribution: there is a lower bound at 0, the distribution is skewed near the bound, only whole numbers occur, and in the middle it is roughly symmetric. That is why Binomial or Beta-Binomial is used, even though the answers do not really arise from identical Bernoulli trials. If people differ in ability, the sums scatter more than under the binomial distribution. Then Beta-Binomial fits better.

Here is what such a comparison can look like: for the sum scores of a questionnaire, you can fit Gaussian, Student-t, Beta-Binomial, and an ordinal probit model across all items. Often they give almost the same means. Gaussian and Student-t drop out anyway, because their prediction intervals can contain values outside the possible range. The probit model is the most laborious. Beta-Binomial can be skewed, gives whole-number predictions, and is much simpler, so it is enough here. Other researchers might decide differently.

As a yardstick: right enough that no implausible results arise, and simple enough that you can implement the model sensibly.
"All models are wrong, but some are useful."George Box
With Bayes this is often easier, even with unusual link functions. The model works on the link scale, but statements are usually needed on the response scale, that is, in the original range of values. For this, you transform every single draw from the posterior distribution back, averaged over covariates or random effects where needed (marginalizing). The uncertainty is thus carried over directly to the response scale, and comparisons of means or slopes can be evaluated inferentially right away, for example via intervals or the probability that a difference is greater than zero. In frequentist statistics this needs an extra step: the delta method, simulation, or bootstrapping.
How the tool uses both
Stage 0 clarifies up front what the likelihood describes and what the raw data can reliably show. Stage 1 follows the first way: questions about the generating process narrow down the candidates. Stage 2 follows the second way: you compare the candidates on the data (AIC/BIC). Stage 3 shows how to implement the result in brms. In the end, there is a reasoned trade-off, not a recipe.

The choice of distribution family (likelihood function) for your own data can be narrowed down considerably with a few targeted questions and considerations. Different data-generating processes lead to different distributions of the data: reaction times, sum scores from test items, and count data with a heterogeneous rate each produce characteristic shapes that can be predicted before the first observation, if the generating process is known.

Important: The likelihood describes the distribution of the outcome depending on the predictors, not the raw values as a whole. In the simplest case the model has only an intercept and describes the distribution and shape of the outcome itself. Once predictors are added, it is about the conditional distributions, that is, the distribution at fixed values of the predictors. The tools Conditional Distributions and GLM in 3D illustrate this. The histogram of the raw data can therefore be misleading. It reliably shows the range of values (data type, bounds, zeros), but the shape only within the model. An example to try out is in Stage 0.

How to read this overview▸
Required parameters are the quantities brms needs to know for the chosen family. Some get a value automatically without writing anything into the model formula for them (e.g. σ for Student-t) — brms estimates them in the background as a single global value for the whole dataset. Others must appear explicitly in the formula, such as n for Binomial via trials(n).

Priors are starting assumptions you can optionally set via prior(). Without your own specification, brms uses sensible internal defaults — the priors suggested here are deliberately weakly informative: not "everything is equally plausible" (flat), but also not a rigid prescription. They are rough starting points for the respective distribution family, not finished answers — which prior actually makes sense always depends on the scale and context of your own data. To explore and adjust: Prior Lab.

Extensions (distributional models) means: not just the expected value (μ, λ, p, …) may depend on predictors — auxiliary parameters like spread, dispersion, or the share of structural zeros can also get their own formula with their own predictors, e.g. when two groups vary in spread to different degrees.

Priors apply to model parameters, not to the raw values. The intercept is the value (on the link scale) when all predictors = 0. Center the predictors so that this is a meaningful point. class = b applies to each slope, that is, the effect of a predictor, not the level of the raw values. Whether a prior fits your data is checked with a prior predictive check, not by looking at the histogram.
💡Good to know
Free flexibility: the one-parameter trick
Student-t instead of Normal, Negative Binomial instead of Poisson, Beta-Binomial instead of Binomial: in all three cases the more robust family adds one extra parameter to the standard model. This parameter controls how far the model may deviate from the simpler distribution — for instance through outliers or overdispersion. When that extra flexibility isn't needed, the model automatically approaches the standard case (ν → ∞, φ → ∞).
No rigid scheme
The mapping in Stage 1 is a well-reasoned starting hypothesis, not a law. Under certain conditions — large n, clean measurement conditions, few outliers, interest in the mean rather than the tails — the classical standard (Normal, Poisson, Binomial) remains a legitimate, simpler choice. If several candidates in Stage 2 converge to practically the same result, that is itself an informative answer: the family didn't matter for this particular question.

Overview

This tool supports a decision that comes before every regression: Which distribution family (likelihood) fits my data? Most people reach for Normal, Poisson, or Binomial because they are the best known, not because they fit best. A conscious look at data type and generating process takes a few minutes and prevents an unsuitable likelihood from silently distorting estimates, standard errors, or predictions.

The four stages build on each other, but you can jump via the cards at the top at any time.

  • Stage 0 — Conditional distribution: Why the raw histogram is not the right quantity.
  • Stage 1 — Data-generating process: Questions about the generating process lead to a candidate list.
  • Stage 2 — Compare candidates on data: on simulated example data or your own data. The candidates are fitted as a regression model and judged conditionally.
  • Stage 3 — Implementing in brms: for reference: parametrization, priors, and checks per family. The basics of priors are covered in the Prior Compass.

The core idea: conditional distribution

The likelihood describes the distribution of the outcome y depending on the predictors, that is, at the same predictor values (e.g. within one group). The raw histogram of all values mixes all predictor values. Two groups with normally distributed values each give a combined histogram with two peaks or a broad hump, although Normal is the right likelihood.

What you can still see reliably in the raw data

  • Integers (counts, sum scores) or continuous values.
  • Value range: positive and negative values (theoretically unbounded), only positive values, values in (0, 1), hard upper and lower bounds.
  • Point masses: unusually many exact zeros (or ones), clearly more than a single distribution would lead you to expect. For counts, only the comparison with the model decides this.

The shape of the distribution (skew, heavy tails, spread) you judge only in the model, from residuals and the predictive check. A model without a predictor (just an overall mean) is a special case in which the raw histogram really is the right quantity.

Stage 1: Data-generating process

You answer a few questions and get a candidate list with reasoning.

  • Answer conditionally: Think of the values under fixed conditions (within a group), not the histogram of all values.
  • "I don't know" is allowed. You then get a robust starting point that Stages 2 and 3 check.
  • Cards: Default is the recommended start, Alternative a justified option, Classic (naive) the well-known choice with its limits.
  • "Not a law" says when the simpler family is enough. "Typical trap" names the most common wrong conclusion drawn from the raw histogram. For repeated measures, a note points to random intercepts.

Reaction times and other positive values

Whether positive, right-skewed values come from a multiplicative process (effects in percent) or a drift up to a threshold can usually not be decided from the data alone. Lognormal, Gamma, and Inverse Gaussian often differ little at the same predictor values. Theory, research question, and the conditional checks in Stages 2 and 3 decide.

Sums of items

Sums of 0/1 items fit Binomial or Beta-Binomial directly. Sums of Likert items can be modelled approximately the same way (trials(N·(k−1))), but the points of one item are not independent. The approximation has to be checked conditionally; an ordinal model at item level is cleaner.

Stage 2: Compare candidates on data

Each candidate is fitted by maximum likelihood as a regression model, corresponding to y ~ x in brms with the matching family. In the simulated example x is a group; with your own data it can also be a continuous predictor. The question is: does the distribution fit at a fixed predictor value?

Controls

  • Scenario: cards with a plain description, an example and the technical term (e.g. "more spread than expected" = overdispersion). The scenario determines the simulated data and the candidates.
  • N (items per sum): only for sums, the number of items.
  • Group difference: strength of the group effect. New data are generated when you release the slider.
  • Include group / predictor x in the model: switching it off shows the model without a predictor. With your own data the predictor is called "x", because it can also be continuous. The group difference then ends up as spread in the model, and fit and residuals get worse although the family stays the same.
  • ＋ Normal for comparison: additionally fits the normal distribution, the standard likelihood many people know. You see when it fits poorly (impossible predictions, wrong shape) and when it is good enough. For whole-number data it is judged by the probability of the interval y ± 0.5, so the AIC stays comparable. The table column "impossible" shows the share of impossible predictions.
  • Generate new data draws a new sample, Paste your own data takes your values: one row per observation, either just y or y x (x = group 0/1 or a continuous predictor), separated by spaces, commas, or tabs.

If the value range of your data does not fit a candidate (e.g. negative values for Lognormal), it is skipped with a note.

The three views

  1. Data and model prediction: Dots are the observations. For each candidate you see the predicted mean and the 90% prediction interval, the range in which the model expects 90% of new observations. About one in ten points may fall outside; clearly more or fewer points outside indicates wrong spread.
  2. Predictive check (like pp_check): Grey bars show the raw values, lines the values the fitted model predicts including the group. The raw histogram only says something in comparison with these.
  3. Residual check: Quantile residuals as a QQ plot, see the next section.

AIC / BIC

Smaller is better. Both reward fit and penalize additional parameters (BIC more strongly). If candidates are less than about 2 ΔAIC apart, the choice hardly matters for these data. The table is a quick comparison with the same predictor part, not a substitute for a proper model comparison (Stage 3, loo_compare). Only compare families that predict the same values, not count with continuous models.

Quantile residuals

The idea

For each observation the model asks: Where does the observed value lie in the distribution I predicted for this observation (at its predictor values)? The answer is a value u between 0 and 1, the cumulative probability F(y | x). If the model is right, u is uniformly distributed, and qnorm(u) is standard normal. These are exactly the quantile residuals (Dunn & Smyth, 1996).

Why not ordinary residuals? Raw or Pearson residuals are neither normal nor equally spread for Poisson, Binomial, Gamma, or Beta, even when the model is correct. Quantile residuals have the same target appearance for every family, so all candidates can be compared on the same yardstick. The same idea is behind DHARMa::simulateResiduals() and the PIT plots in bayesplot (e.g. pp_check(fit, type = "loo_pit_qq")).

How the tool computes them

  • From the fitted model, 400 values are simulated for each observation. The share of simulated values below the observed one gives u.
  • For discrete data (counts, sums) the distribution function is a staircase. Then u is drawn at random within the step (randomized quantile residuals). The points therefore jiggle slightly between redraws. Look at the pattern, not at single points.
  • The plot: the x axis shows the theoretical normal quantiles, the y axis the sorted residuals. The dashed diagonal is the target: the model fits if the points lie on it.

Reading the patterns

On the diagonalThe distribution fits at a fixed predictor value. Nothing to do.
S shape: heavy tailsThe ends move away from the diagonal. There are more extreme values than predicted. Student-t instead of Normal, Negative Binomial instead of Poisson for counts.
Reverse S shape: light tailsFewer extreme values than predicted. The spread is modelled too large or the range is bounded (consider Beta, Binomial).
Arc: skewOne end is too long, the other too short. Consider a skewed family (Lognormal, Gamma, Skew-Normal) or a log link.
Too steep: too little spreadThe residuals spread more than N(0, 1). The model underestimates the spread, typical of overdispersion. Flatter than the diagonal means the spread is overestimated.
Shifted in parallel: meanThe model is systematically off, e.g. because a predictor is missing (try the "Include group in the model" switch).
Single points at the ends are usually outliers. With small n (below about 30) the ends fluctuate strongly even when the model is right. Then judge the middle and the overall pattern.

What quantile residuals do not show

  • A missing structure (nonlinear effect, missing predictor) is better seen when you plot the residuals against the predictor.
  • A QQ plot checks the shape of the distribution, not the independence of observations. With repeated measures from the same person you additionally need random effects.
  • They are a check, not a test with a fixed threshold. The judgement becomes reliable together with the predictive check and the prediction interval.

Stage 3: Implementing in brms

The workflow is shown at the top: set priors (get_prior()), prior predictive check (sample_prior = "only"), fit the model (brm()), posterior predictive check (pp_check()), compare families (loo_compare()). For each family you find:

  • Required parameters: What brms knows for the family, what is estimated automatically, and what must go into the formula explicitly (e.g. trials(n)).
  • Priors: weakly informative starting points. They apply to model parameters, not to the raw values. The intercept is the value (on the link scale) when all predictors = 0, so center the predictors. class = b applies per slope.
  • Go-to default and extensions (distributional models, e.g. sigma ~ group).
  • Check after fitting: suitable pp_check calls for the family.

To explore priors: Prior Lab. For all families in detail: Distribution Lab. The brms Model Builder generates ready-made model code.

Terms

  • Predictor: variable meant to explain y (group, dose, age).
  • Link scale: scale on which the linear model works (identity, log, logit). Coefficients act additively there, and as a factor on the original scale for log.
  • Prediction interval: range for new observations, wider than an interval for the mean.
  • Residual: deviation of the observation from the model prediction, here as a quantile residual on the normal scale.
  • Overdispersion: more spread at the same predictor values than the family allows.
  • Maximum likelihood vs. Bayes: Stage 2 fits by maximum likelihood (fast, no priors). Stage 3 describes the Bayesian model in brms.

Limits of the tool

  • The simulations use a binary group; your own data can contain a continuous predictor. Interactions, several predictors, and random effects are not fitted.
  • Hurdle and zero-inflation models and the shifted lognormal are described in Stage 3 but cannot be fitted in Stage 2.
  • The Stage 2 fits are maximum-likelihood approximations, not a substitute for a Bayesian model with a prior and posterior predictive check.
