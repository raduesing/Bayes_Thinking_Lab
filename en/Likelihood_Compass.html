Likelihood Compass — which distribution family fits your data-generating process?  ·  Not "what does the histogram look like?" — but "how did these numbers come to be?"

The choice of distribution family (likelihood function) for your own data can be narrowed down considerably with a few targeted questions and considerations. Different data-generating processes lead to different distributions of the data: reaction times, sum scores from test items, and count data with a heterogeneous rate each produce characteristic shapes that can be predicted before the first observation, if the generating process is known.

How to read this overview▸
Required parameters are the quantities brms needs to know for the chosen family. Some get a value automatically without writing anything into the model formula for them (e.g. σ for Student-t) — brms estimates them in the background as a single global value for the whole dataset. Others must appear explicitly in the formula, such as n for Binomial via trials(n).

Priors are starting assumptions you can optionally set via prior(). Without your own specification, brms uses sensible internal defaults — the priors suggested here are deliberately weakly informative: not "everything is equally plausible" (flat), but also not a rigid prescription.

Extensions (distributional models) means: not just the expected value (μ, λ, p, …) may depend on predictors — auxiliary parameters like spread, dispersion, or the share of structural zeros can also get their own formula with their own predictors, e.g. when two groups vary in spread to different degrees.
These priors are rough starting points for the respective distribution family, not finished answers — which prior actually makes sense always depends on the scale and context of your own data. To explore and adjust: Prior Lab.
Free flexibility: the one-parameter trick
Student-t instead of Normal, Negative Binomial instead of Poisson, Beta-Binomial instead of Binomial — in all three cases the more robust family costs only one extra parameter, which automatically converges to the simpler family's limiting case (ν → ∞, φ → ∞) for "well-behaved" data. There is practically no downside to starting with the more flexible family directly — only the possibility of catching unnoticed overdispersion or outliers.
No rigid scheme
The mapping in Stage 1 is a well-reasoned starting hypothesis, not a law. Under certain conditions — large n, clean measurement conditions, few outliers, interest in the mean rather than the tails — the classical standard (Normal, Poisson, Binomial) remains a legitimate, simpler choice. If several candidates in Stage 2 converge to practically the same result, that is itself an informative answer: the family didn't matter for this particular question.

Prior Predictive Check
Before looking at real data: simulates datasets purely from distribution family + priors. Does the combination generate plausible values — or does it predict impossible magnitudes, negative reaction times, or proportions > 1? A wrong likelihood type often shows up here, before any fitting happens.
📈
Posterior Predictive Check
After estimation: simulates datasets from the fitted model and compares them directly against the real data. This is where a wrong likelihood becomes most visible — the simulations systematically miss the skew, bounds, or spread of the real data.
⚖
LOO Lab — Model Comparison
The actual referee: Leave-One-Out cross-validation estimates how well each model predicts unseen data — not just how well it traces the training data. Two families that were close together in Stage 2 can be pitted directly against each other here.

What will I learn here?
This tool helps with a decision that comes up before every regression: which distribution family fits the data at hand — reasoned from knowing how the numbers came to be, not just what their histogram looks like.
  • What data-generating process sits behind continuous measurements, count data, sum scores, or proportions — and which family follows from each?
  • Why does a more robust family (Student-t, Negative Binomial, Beta-Binomial) cost almost nothing, yet potentially save a lot?
  • When does the classical standard still suffice?
The three stages
  • Stage 1 — Data-generating process: A handful of targeted questions about the generating process lead to a short candidate list with reasoning.
  • Stage 2 — Check on your own data: The candidates are fit by maximum likelihood to synthetic example data (or your own pasted values) — histogram/frequencies, fitted curves, and an AIC/BIC comparison side by side.
  • Stage 3 — Model comparison & predictive checks: Links to Prior Predictive Check, Posterior Predictive Check, and LOO Lab — the actual tools for a robust decision.
Why this matters
Most people reach for Normal, Poisson, or Binomial automatically — not because these families fit best, but because they're the most familiar. A deliberate look at the generating process costs little and prevents an ill-fitting likelihood from silently biasing estimates, standard errors, or predictions.
